## 1. Market Context

The UK quick-sale/cash-buyer sector remains small relative to the wider housing market. The last authoritative sizing exercise, the Office of Fair Trading's 2013 "Quick House Sales" market study, estimated the sector at 0.5%-1% of all UK residential transactions by volume, worth roughly £0.5bn-£0.9bn a year, with sellers typically forgoing 10%-25% of open-market value (and the OFT noting it had seen "much higher" discounts in some cases)[^1]. No public body has re-run this exercise since, so there is **no reliable up-to-date estimate of sector size** — any current claim of market value should be treated as an estimate rather than fact.

Structural conditions that feed the sector have intensified over the past 24 months. Mortgage possession claims in England and Wales reached their highest level since 2019 in 2025, and possession orders hit their highest level since 2014, with the Ministry of Justice reporting quarterly claims fluctuating between roughly 4,400 and 6,800 through 2025 and into 2026[^2][^3]. Residential sale fall-throughs also stayed elevated: TwentyCi recorded 303,551 collapsed transactions in 2025 (23.8% of agreed sales), up from 268,045 (21.9%) in 2019[^4], while a separate TwentyCi cut for Modern Lender put the Q1 2026 national fall-through rate at 23.7%, down marginally from 24% in Q1 2025[^5]. These figures replace the "roughly 1 in 3" figure sometimes quoted in older marketing material — see Section 9 for the full fact-check.

## 2. Segment Deep-Dives

### Probate

An estimated 239,091 grants of probate were issued in England & Wales in 2025 (down 10% on 2024), from 242,821 applications, per Ministry of Justice/HMCTS Family Court Statistics — the primary official measure[^6][^7]. Roughly 81% of grants of representation are full grants of probate (the balance being letters of administration where there was no will)[^7]. **Could not verify** a current, sourced figure for the specific proportion of estates that include a residential property; this figure should be treated as unconfirmed until a primary source (e.g., HMRC IHT statistics or Land Registry) is found. Probate property sales also take markedly longer through conventional channels: one conveyancing-timeline analysis put average probate sale timelines at 16-24 weeks from offer, versus 8-10 weeks for a straightforward chain-free sale[^8] — a gap directly relevant to Kept's promise of completion flexed to the grant date. Executors are typically not selling for themselves; they act on behalf of beneficiaries and are often managing bereavement, legal duties, and family dynamics simultaneously, which shapes the calm, drama-free tone the brand already specifies. Bereavement-sector guidance (see Section 8) consistently stresses being led by the bereaved person's pace and avoiding pressure — directly transferable to messaging for this segment.

### Chain Breaks

There is no dedicated national statistic for "chain breaks" as a category, but the elevated fall-through rate (23.7%-23.8% of agreed sales nationally in 2025/26) is the best available proxy for how frequently a chain is put at risk by a buyer or seller elsewhere in the chain pulling out[^4][^5]. TwentyCi's regional breakdown shows fall-through rates falling in 10 of 13 UK regions year-on-year but rising in Inner London (24.6% to 27.0%)[^5], suggesting chain-break pressure is not uniform and that Northern English regions (Kept's patch) may see somewhat more stable, if still material, fall-through exposure than London. For a seller mid-chain, the triggering event is typically sudden — a buyer's mortgage falls through, a survey down-values, or a linked buyer withdraws — meaning urgency is externally imposed rather than self-generated, which fits Kept's "step in so the chain holds" positioning.

### Mortgage-Refused / Down-Valued Sellers

This segment overlaps mechanically with market-wide down-valuation and affordability issues but has no separate public dataset; TwentyCi's fall-through analysis captures some but not all of these cases within the broader 23.8% figure[^4]. **Could not verify** a specific UK statistic isolating "survey down-valuation" as a fall-through cause; existing industry commentary attributes fall-throughs broadly to chain breaks, finance issues, and survey findings without a standardised breakdown that could be sourced reliably in this research pass.

### Separation and Divorce

Family Court Statistics show 28,890 divorce applications under the no-fault divorce legislation in Q1 2025 alone (down 3% year-on-year), with 22,053 final orders granted in the same quarter (down 14% year-on-year)[^9]. ONS's divorce dataset provides longer-run annual figures and rates but a full-year 2024/2025 release was still pending consolidation at the time of this research[^10][^11]. **Could not verify** what proportion of divorcing couples own a home jointly; this is a plausible assumption based on UK homeownership rates but not a sourced statistic. Divorce sales are procedurally distinct in that two solicitors, not one seller, typically drive the transaction, and Kept's stated approach ("solicitors talk to solicitors") maps directly onto this dynamic.

### Relocation

UK emigration has remained elevated: ONS estimates roughly 246,000 British nationals left the UK in the year to December 2025, similar to the 257,000 (revised) who left the year before[^12]. This is a national figure covering all emigrants, not homeowners specifically, so it should be read as directional context rather than a precise addressable-market estimate for Kept's segment. Relocation sellers are commonly time-boxed by a job start date, school term, or visa condition rather than financial distress, meaning the "sign once, complete when needed" flexibility is the primary value driver rather than price certainty per se.

### Repossession Risk

Mortgage possession activity rose sharply through the middle of 2025 before easing: claims in Q1 2025 were 6,765 (up 31% year-on-year), fell to 4,439 by Q4 2025 (down 27% year-on-year), and continued falling in Q1 2026 to 5,181 (Q1 2026 comparison is against a revised Q1 2025 base of 6,767)[^2][^13][^14]. Actual repossessions by county court bailiffs, however, moved the other way in places — 5,160 mortgaged properties were repossessed across 2025, a 39% rise on 2024 and the highest annual total since 2019[^2]. Median time from claim to repossession is now 46.7 weeks (Q4 2025), up from 46.0 weeks a year earlier[^2], indicating that most homeowners at this stage have a longer runway than headline urgency implies — supporting Kept's stated preference to decline sales that would leave unpayable debt and to signpost StepChange/Citizens Advice rather than compete on speed alone.

### Problem Property

Short leases: an estimated 246,000 owner-occupied households in England were living in homes with a lease of 80 years or fewer as of 2024-25, roughly 10% of leasehold owner-occupiers[^15], with mortgage viability deteriorating steeply below 70-80 years remaining[^16][^17]. Cladding: as at July 2026, 4,697 residential buildings 11m+ in England were being monitored for unsafe cladding, housing an estimated 250,000 homes, of which around 39% had completed remediation; separately, the Leasehold Knowledge Partnership estimates up to 840,000 leasehold flats in England could be potentially affected by wider building-safety issues[^18]. Knotweed: an industry estimate (not government-verified in this research) puts Japanese knotweed prevalence at around 5% of UK properties[^19], while a 2019 parliamentary briefing citing a 2010 CABI estimate put affected residential properties at around 1.25% and total annual UK cost (2018 prices) at over £200m[^20]. Together (a specialist lender) estimated in a report published 31 July 2026 that more than 1.5 million UK homes — about 6% of the housing stock — sit outside mainstream mortgage lending criteria for reasons including short leases, non-standard construction, cladding, and structural issues[^16][^21]. This is the single most quantifiable "problem property" addressable-market figure found and is recent enough to cite with confidence, though it is a private-sector estimate rather than an official statistic.

Cross-cutting findings from review aggregators: the most consistent negative theme across the sector is **last-minute offer reduction** ("gazundering" in reverse) between initial and final figures, cited by multiple independent guides as the leading source of seller complaints and Property Ombudsman referrals[^27][^34][^25]. ## 4. Positioning Opportunities and Gaps

No identified competitor commits, in writing, to a fixed number of narrow exceptions for renegotiation between offer and exchange; most either stay silent on the point or use vaguer language ("no last-minute reductions without RICS evidence," which still leaves room for discretion)[^32]. Kept's explicit three-exception model is a genuine differentiator against a sector where "dropping the offer near completion" is the single most cited complaint pattern[^34][^25]. Similarly, few competitors state clearly that no offer is given without a physical viewing — most marketing leads with speed ("offer within 24 hours" or "within minutes")[^35][^36], which is efficient marketing but is also exactly the promise pattern the ASA found misleading in at least one historic ruling[^28]. Kept's "no offer without a viewing" stance is slower to communicate but more defensible and differentiated, and directly rebuts the "how can they price it without seeing it" scepticism visible in seller-facing guides[^37][^25].

The sector's near-universal reliance on 75%-85% of market value as the pricing band is treated by comparison sites as a known, accepted trade-off rather than a point of differentiation[^23][^38][^34], so competing on numeric offer percentage is unlikely to be productive; competing on process transparency, redress-scheme behaviour, and segment-specific empathy is the more defensible lane, particularly since Kept's declared segments (probate, chain break, separation, relocation, repossession risk, problem property) are rarely marketed as distinct journeys by competitors, who instead use one generic funnel for "any" seller[^35][^39][^36]. The named "we won't buy any home" framing is a further point of difference: virtually every competitor researched uses "we buy any house/home" branding, which is also the exact phrase the 2013 OFT study and subsequent consumer guidance treat with suspicion[^38][^1].

## 5. Trust and Reputation

Consumer-facing guides converge on a consistent list of what sellers fear: below-market offers dropped further at the last minute, hidden or upfront fees, high-pressure tactics to sign quickly, "option agreement" structures mis-sold as direct purchases, untraceable company ownership, and manipulated online reviews[^25][^34][^38]. One independent guide states plainly that cash-buying is "not regulated," that Property Ombudsman membership is voluntary redress rather than regulation, and that NAPB "is not a redress scheme or a regulator" — language Kept's own credentials section already echoes[^34]. The trust signals that these guides repeatedly recommend checking are: proof of funds confirmed by the seller's own solicitor, TPO/NAPB/Property Redress Scheme membership verified independently (not just claimed), a straight-purchase contract (not an option agreement), no upfront fees, and a written completion-window commitment[^25][^34].

The regulatory backdrop has not fundamentally changed since the OFT's 2013 study, which found the sector "does not always operate in the best interests of consumers" and highlighted low-balling, pressure selling, and misleading practices[^1]. No statutory regulator has since taken over direct oversight of quick-sale firms; the sector instead relies on voluntary membership of NAPB and/or The Property Ombudsman (now largely the Property Redress Scheme/TPO landscape), alongside general consumer protection law (Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations) and ASA/CAP advertising rules[^34][^40]. The ASA has taken enforcement action in this specific sector before — a 2014 ruling against We Buy Any House found ads misleading for implying direct purchase when the firm often acted as an intermediary, ordering the ads withdrawn in their existing form[^28]. This confirms that regulatory risk in this category is real and precedented, reinforcing the case for Kept's plain-English "not FCA-regulated, we never use the word advice" disclosure.

## 6. Organic and Referral Landscape

Search results for "cash house buyers," "sell my house fast," and "probate property sale" in the UK are dominated by third-party comparison and review sites (e.g., Ready Steady Sell, HouseBought4Cash, The Advisory, Springbok, resident.com, propertyinvestmentproject.co.uk) rather than by the operating companies themselves, meaning much of the top-of-funnel discovery layer for this category is intermediated by affiliate-style content[^23][^24][^41][^22]. MoneySavingExpert's own house-buying content is generally aimed at buyers/mortgages rather than covering cash house-buying companies directly, and its forum shows unmoderated peer discussion warning that "with many/most house-buying companies, it's more complicated than [a simple sale]" and urging seekers to have any agreement reviewed by a solicitor[^42]. StepChange's repossession and mortgage-arrears guidance does not name or link to any commercial cash-buyer brand; it instead recommends contacting the lender, seeking a "assisted voluntary sale scheme" (lender-run) or Breathing Space, and signposts Shelter for housing-specific advice — meaning organic goodwill from debt charities is not something Kept (or any competitor) can currently expect to win by SEO alone, but the charity's own guidance validates Kept's segment 6 policy of declining sales that would leave unresolvable debt and always signposting StepChange/Citizens Advice[^43][^44]. On referral fees, the SRA Code of Conduct explicitly permits solicitors to receive referral fees from third parties (including property buyers) provided the arrangement is disclosed in writing to the client, the client gives informed consent, and the fee is not linked to criminal proceedings[^45][^46]; separate National Trading Standards guidance for estate agents imposes parallel written-disclosure obligations under the Estate Agents Act 1979 and consumer protection rules, though this framework technically governs agents rather than solicitors[^47].

Manchester carries the heaviest visible density of competing cash-buyer marketing among Kept's three target cities: national brand We Buy Any Home runs a dedicated Manchester landing page claiming to be "the number one cash buying company in Manchester" with offers within minutes[^35], while Housebuyers4u, Ziphouse (which runs at least two near-duplicate Manchester-targeted pages), and Springmove also actively target the city with local-sounding "we buy any house Manchester" content[^30][^39][^48][^32][^49]. HouseBought4Cash also maintains a dedicated Greater Manchester probate-sales page, explicitly positioning around inherited property across Manchester, Stockport, Bolton, Oldham, Rochdale, Salford and Wigan — directly overlapping Kept's stated probate priority segment and postcode patch[^50]. **Could not verify**, within this research pass, specific local press or community-media advertising rates for Manchester, Leeds, or Sheffield, or a ranked list of the most prominent probate solicitor firms in the target postcodes (M14/M19/M20/M21, SK4/SK5/SK7/SK8, LS1/LS6/LS8/LS17, S1/S7/S11/S17); this would require targeted follow-up research directly with local media sales contacts and Law Society "Find a Solicitor" filtering by postcode and probate specialism, which was outside the scope of the sources gathered here.

## 8. Language Guidance

Bereavement-sector guidance consistently prioritises being led by the bereaved person's own pace, validating rather than minimising emotion, and avoiding scripted reassurance in favour of specific, low-pressure offers of help; Marie Curie's public-facing counselling guidance, for example, stresses "let them express their emotions, whatever they are," avoiding pity in favour of compassion, and asking directly "what do you need from me" rather than assuming[^51]. Debt-charity guidance from StepChange is similarly non-alarmist: it frames repossession as "always a last resort," emphasises that a lot of time exists before it happens, and consistently recommends practical, sequenced next steps (talk to the lender, build a budget, seek free advice) rather than urgency-based language[^44][^43]. Both bodies of guidance validate the brand voice Kept has already specified — calm certainty, no timers, no exclamation marks — and suggest specific copy techniques: naming the exact next step rather than a vague call to action, quantifying timelines honestly (weeks, not "instantly"), and explicitly stating what the seller is not obligated to do at each stage. **Could not verify**, within available sources, a definitive list of "UK financial brands widely considered to do empathetic marketing well" from an independent industry ranking; this claim would benefit from a follow-up search focused on marketing-trade press (Marketing Week, Campaign) rather than the property-sector sources gathered in this pass.
