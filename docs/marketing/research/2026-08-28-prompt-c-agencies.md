## 3. Probate Direct Mail Providers

No specific UK direct-mail provider explicitly marketing "probate data" or "bereavement mailing lists" post any 2019 ICO enforcement action was identified with a verifiable, current compliance statement in this research pass. Smee & Ford was found to state on its own site that it processes "the name and postal address (where provided) of executors and or beneficiaries contained within UK wills and grants of probate" under a UK GDPR framework[^14], but **the specific details of a 2019 ICO enforcement action involving this company, and what compliance changes resulted, could not be verified** from sources available in this pass — this claim requires direct verification against the ICO's public enforcement register (ico.org.uk) before Kept treats Smee & Ford or any similar provider as compliant, and before any commercial use of probate-derived personal data for direct marketing is considered, given clear UK GDPR purpose-limitation risk in reusing probate/will records for unrelated commercial solicitation[^15].

On the separate question of respecting the deceased and their families in outbound mail, the Royal Mail Bereavement Register and the Mailing Preference Service exist specifically to **suppress** unwanted marketing mail to a deceased person's address, and the Royal Mail's own bereavement redirection service is the mechanism executors use to have post forwarded[^16] — these are relevant to Kept in the opposite direction: as a discipline for excluding any address flagged via these registers from outbound prospecting, rather than as a data-sourcing channel. No general-purpose UK direct-mail house with a demonstrated, named specialism in bereavement or probate-sensitive sectors was identified in this research pass; this is a further gap requiring direct outreach to established direct-mail fulfilment houses (e.g. via the Direct Marketing Association's member directory) with explicit questions about their probate-data sourcing practices and lawful basis, none of which could be verified here.

## 4. Rate Benchmarks

PPC management pricing in the UK converges consistently across multiple 2025–2026 sources on a tiered structure by ad-spend level. For accounts spending £1,000–£3,000/month on media (Kept's exact starting bracket), typical UK guidance points to a freelancer or specialist consultant at roughly £400–£800/month flat fee, or a percentage model of 15–20% of spend (roughly £150–£600/month on a £1,000 budget)[^17][^18][^19]. Some flat-fee agencies explicitly serving small budgets quote considerably lower entry tiers — one provider lists a £145/month tier for ad spend up to £500 and £295/month for spend up to £2,500[^20], though these low-cost tiers typically involve templated setups and less senior attention. The regional data point most relevant to Kept: PPC agencies in the North West of England average roughly £950/month against £1,200/month near London, and Yorkshire & Humber averages around £880/month, indicating Northern England providers are typically 15–25% cheaper than London equivalents for comparable service levels[^21][^22].

For SEO and long-form content, ProCopywriters' own pricing guidance states UK freelance copywriters charge a day rate between £300 and £1,800, with a median of £440/day[^23] — for probate/chain-break/problem-property guide content requiring research and a strict editorial bar, a specialist writer at or above the median day rate is the realistic benchmark, likely translating to roughly £300–£600 per substantial long-form article depending on research depth and revisions (this per-article estimate is derived from the day-rate figure, not a directly published per-article rate, and should be confirmed with each writer).

For direct mail, per-piece all-in costs (data, print, fulfilment, postage) for a standard addressed A5/postcard campaign were found in the £0.30–£2.50 range depending on volume and personalisation, with a worked 10,000-piece example costing around £0.43 per piece[^24][^25]; at Kept's likely 500–2,000 piece volumes, unit costs are typically higher than the 10,000-piece rate due to lost economies of scale on setup and data costs, so a realistic working assumption is £0.60–£1.50 per piece all-in at this smaller volume, though this is an extrapolation rather than a directly sourced small-volume rate card.

**Fractional/part-time marketing lead day-rates in Northern England could not be verified** within this research pass — no source specifically addressed fractional CMO or part-time marketing-lead day rates broken out by Northern England geography; this would benefit from a direct search of fractional-marketing marketplaces (e.g. Fractional, Digital Marketing Institute rate surveys) in a follow-up pass.

## 5. Selection Questions, Contract Norms, and Red Flags

### Ten questions for a first call

1. Can you show two live or recent clients in property, legal, financial services, or another regulated/sensitive sector, and can we speak to one as a reference?
2. How do you handle advertising a brand that must never show urgency tactics, countdown timers, or a price/valuation figure in the ad or on the landing page?
3. What is your experience with Google's and Meta's housing-related ad policies, including Special Ad Category restrictions on Meta?
4. How would you structure campaigns to keep cost-per-qualified-lead under £250 at a £1,000/month starting budget, and when would you recommend increasing spend?
5. Who owns the Google Ads account, GA4 property, and any tracking numbers if we end the relationship — and can you confirm this in writing before we start?
6. What is your process for writing about bereavement, financial distress, or repossession risk without exploiting or minimising it?
7. Have you, or has your business, ever run "we buy any house"-style urgency-led creative, and if so, what did you learn from it?
8. What does your fee actually include month to month (hours, deliverables, reporting cadence), and is it a flat fee or percentage of spend?
9. What is your notice period, and is there any minimum-term tie-in beyond a standard rolling notice?
10. How do you measure incrementality or attribution accuracy at low monthly lead volumes (single digits per week), given formal lift-testing isn't statistically practical at this scale?

### Contract norms to insist on

Insist on a 30-day rolling notice period rather than any fixed-term minimum commitment, full ownership and admin access to the Google Ads account, GA4 property, Meta Business Manager, and any call-tracking numbers under Kept's own accounts (not the agency's manager account) from day one, a written scope of work with explicit monthly deliverables rather than vague "ongoing optimisation" language, and a data processing agreement compliant with UK GDPR covering any personal data (including probate-derived data, if used) the provider handles on Kept's behalf.

### Red flags specific to this sector

Treat as red flags: any guarantee of "page one rankings" or a specific ranking position (a practice widely flagged as a misleading-claims risk under general UK marketing-agency guidance), a portfolio history dominated by urgency-led or "we buy any house"-style creative given the sector's known trust problems (see prior research on ASA rulings and consumer-guide warnings)[^26][^27], "content farm" delivery models that produce generic, unresearched articles rather than plain-English guides written to a strict editorial bar, reluctance to discuss GDPR/data-sourcing compliance specifically for any probate or bereavement-adjacent data use, and any provider unwilling to grant Kept direct ownership of ad accounts and tracking assets from the outset.
