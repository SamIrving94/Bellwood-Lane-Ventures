## 2. Policy Constraints

Google classifies housing ads under its Personalized Advertising policy rather than a manual "special category" checkbox; Google's automated classifiers detect housing content and retroactively restrict targeting, which can cause "mid-campaign performance drops without warning" if an account is misclassified[^18]. The formally documented restriction under this policy — Google's Restricted Targeting in Personalised Advertising rules — bans use of gender, age, parental status, marital status, and ZIP/postcode-level targeting for housing (and employment and credit) ads, and requires all demographic options to be set to "Enable" rather than excluded, though this enforcement is explicitly scoped to the United States and Canada in Google's own documentation[^19][^20][^21]. **Could not verify**, within available sources, whether Google enforces an equivalent restriction for UK-targeted housing ads as a matter of policy text (the primary Google support pages found apply the hard restrictions to US/Canada only), so Kept should treat US-style housing restrictions as best-practice guidance rather than assume automatic identical enforcement in the UK, and should still expect Google's content classifiers to flag "housing" content broadly regardless of geography.

Meta requires advertisers running housing-related ads (including "housing repairs," "home equity," "appraisal services," and closely related categories) to declare a "Special Ad Category: Housing," which locks age targeting to 18–65+, removes detailed interest/exclusion targeting, and enforces a minimum targeting radius (commonly cited as 15km, with an earlier 10-mile minimum also referenced)[^18][^22]. This directly affects Kept's ability to run tightly-targeted local campaigns in Manchester/Stockport, Leeds, and Sheffield postcodes on Meta, since postcode/ZIP-level and narrow-radius targeting is specifically disallowed under the Housing category[^22][^18]. On remarketing to distress-themed page visitors specifically, no UK-specific regulatory prohibition was found in this research, but the general direction of both platforms' policies (avoiding targeting based on sensitive circumstances, and Google's explicit ban on targeting "sensitive interest categories" in personalised advertising) suggests remarketing lists built from visits to a "repossession" or "bereavement" landing page carry meaningful platform-policy and reputational risk and should rely on broad site-visitor remarketing rather than page-specific distress segments[^19].

## 3. Competitor Ad Teardown

Direct access to the live Google Ads Transparency Center and Meta Ad Library requires interactive browsing tools not available in this research pass, so findings here are drawn from competitors' own landing pages and third-party comparison sites rather than live ad-library pulls; a follow-up session with direct tool access to adstransparency.google.com and the Meta Ad Library is recommended before finalising creative[^23][^24]. Based on landing-page content gathered, the most visible advertisers' claims patterns are: "cash offer within 24 hours" or "within minutes" (We Buy Any Home, Housebuyers4u, Ziphouse)[^25][^15][^17], "no fees, ever" as a near-universal claim across the sector[^13][^15][^26], completion windows quoted as "7–28 days" or "1–3 weeks"[^13][^15][^17], and superlative positioning ("number one," "UK's leading") used without independent substantiation by at least two of the researched brands[^25][^27]. Several local-facing pages (Ziphouse, Housebuyers4u) run near-duplicate city-specific landing pages for Manchester with templated content, suggesting programmatic local-SEO/PPC page generation rather than bespoke local campaigns[^13][^14][^15][^17].

## 4. Compliance: Banned vs Surviving Claims

The one confirmed sector-specific ASA ruling identified in this research is the 2014 finding against We Buy Any House, which held that ads breached CAP Code Rule 3.1 (misleading advertising) by implying the company purchased directly when it often acted as an intermediary/broker to third-party buyers; the ASA ordered the ads not to appear again in that form[^28]. **Could not verify** any more recent (last 24 months) ASA ruling specifically against a UK cash house-buying advertiser for claims such as "up to 100% market value," "sell in 7 days," or "guaranteed offer" — searches of the ASA's public rulings archive did not surface a matching case in this niche within the research window, so Kept should not assume the category has since escaped scrutiny, only that no fresh public precedent was found. The general CAP Code framework most relevant to this category includes Rule 3.1 (must not materially mislead), Rule 3.3 (must not mislead by omitting material information), and Rule 3.9 (qualifying/clarifying statements must be clear), all of which the ASA has applied in adjacent sectors — for example a 2025 ruling against a legal-services firm (Johnson Law Group) for omitting material information about binding e-signature contracts, directly analogous to Kept's own binding-offer terms needing to be stated clearly rather than buried[^29]. Separately, the ASA has ruled that implying a Trustpilot rating that does not reflect reality breaches Rule 3.1[^30], relevant given the sector's frequent use of prominent trust-badge and review-score creative. Beyond ASA/CAP, the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 (CPRs) impose parallel duties not to mislead by action or omission and not to apply aggressive commercial practices (undue pressure) — both squarely relevant to a category where "pressure to sign quickly" is a leading consumer complaint theme identified in prior research[^31][^27]. Wording patterns that appear to have survived scrutiny across the researched competitor set include specific, bounded timescale claims (e.g., "cash offer within 24 hours," "completion in 7–28 days") paired with a stated "no fees" position and visible NAPB/TPO membership badges, rather than unqualified superlative claims[^7][^13][^15]. Given Kept's own fixed, more conservative timings (same-day response, two working days for a written offer, offer held one week, completion in as little as two weeks), all recommended ad copy should state these exact figures and no faster, and should never display a price or valuation figure in the ad itself, consistent with the brand's no-online-figure policy.

## 5. Creative and Landing Benchmarks

No UK property/cash-buyer-specific landing-page conversion benchmark was found in this research pass; the closest available reference points are general UK Google Ads benchmarks showing a cross-industry average Search conversion rate of around 4.4%–4.7%[^1], and a real-estate-specific benchmark (US-sourced) of roughly 3%–3.5% for cash/investor-intent seller campaigns, with a stated cost-per-lead range of $65–$170 (roughly £50–£135 at typical exchange rates, though this is a US market figure and should be treated as directional only)[^5]. On trust elements, prior research into this sector (see the earlier Kept competitor teardown) established that the single largest source of seller distrust is last-minute offer reduction, with proof of funds, verified redress-scheme membership, straight-purchase contracts (not option agreements), and absence of upfront fees being the trust signals consumer guides most consistently recommend checking — meaning landing pages that visibly and verifiably display NAPB/TPO/Property Redress Scheme membership, a named founder or team, and a plainly-stated no-renegotiation policy are likely to convert better than generic "instant offer" pages, though this is an inference from qualitative consumer-guide evidence rather than a measured UK conversion-rate study. **Could not verify** a specific UK study on form-length trade-offs or call-vs-form channel preference by age group for this category; general UX research suggests older or more distressed users (a reasonable proxy for probate executors, many of whom are likely to be older relatives) tend to prefer phone contact over multi-field web forms, but no property-sector-specific UK source substantiating this was found in this research pass, so this should be treated as a plausible hypothesis to test via A/B measurement rather than an established fact.

## 6. Offline Options for Probate

Direct mail response-rate benchmarks from JICMAIL's 2025 Response Rate Tracker (based on roughly 3,800 real UK campaigns) show cold direct mail averaging a 0.9% response rate with a £3.20 return per £1 spent, warm/house-file mail averaging 7.2% response and £9.00 return per £1, and door drops averaging 0.5% response and £2.90 return per £1[^32][^33][^34]. Per-piece all-in costs for a standard addressed campaign (data, print, fulfilment, postage) typically run £0.30–£2.50, with a realistic mid-range example of roughly £0.43 per piece for a 10,000-piece A5 postcard campaign using Royal Mail Advertising Mail rates, implying an all-in campaign cost of around £4,300 for that volume[^35][^36]. At even the lower end of cold response rates (0.9%), a 10,000-piece cold mailing would generate roughly 90 responses at a cost per response of around £48–£64 before further qualification to an actual instructable lead — likely too expensive relative to Kept's sub-£250 target only if response quality is poor, but plausibly efficient if the list is well-targeted to genuine probate triggers.

On data sourcing, the ICO has previously taken enforcement interest in probate-derived direct mail data: Smee & Ford, a specialist data company, has historically compiled information from wills and grants of probate (primarily to notify charities of bequests) and states on its own site that it processes executor/beneficiary names and addresses contained in probate records under GDPR[^37]. **Could not verify**, within sources available in this research pass, the specific details of a 2019 ICO enforcement action against Smee & Ford or what compliance changes resulted; this claim should be independently verified directly against the ICO's public enforcement register before Kept relies on any probate-derived mailing list, and Kept should treat any third-party "probate leads" data source with significant caution pending that verification, given the ICO's general enforcement powers include fines up to £17.5 million or 4% of global turnover for serious data protection breaches[^38]. Grants of probate themselves are public records (available via HM Courts and Tribunals Service application), but bulk commercial use of probate-derived personal data for direct marketing purposes carries clear UK GDPR "purpose limitation" and fair-processing risks that a lawful-basis assessment should address before any mailing campaign is built on this data.

Local press advertising rates for Manchester Evening News, Yorkshire Post, or Sheffield-area titles specifically **could not be verified** within this research pass — general newspaper rate-card data found was not city- or title-specific to Kept's three target areas (one generic community-advertising source cited full-page rates in the tens of thousands of pounds, clearly not representative of local/regional press)[^39]. This is a genuine gap requiring direct outreach to Reach plc (which owns the Manchester Evening News and Yorkshire Post network) or local title sales teams for accurate, current rate cards. Similarly, **could not verify** specific solicitor-facing channel costs (Law Society Gazette advertising rates, local law-society event sponsorship costs) within available sources; this would require direct contact with the Law Society Gazette's commercial team and local law society branches in Greater Manchester, Leeds, and Sheffield.

## 8. Measurement Setup

A UK-GDPR-clean measurement stack at this budget should combine call tracking, form tracking, and a compliant consent framework. Call-tracking tools such as CallRail operate as data processors under UK GDPR (with Kept as controller), require a UK International Data Transfer Addendum given CallRail stores data in the US, and need Kept's own privacy policy to disclose call-recording and cookie use under its legitimate-interest or consent basis[^41][^42]. On consent, Google's Consent Mode v2 became effectively mandatory in March 2024 for sites using Google Ads/Analytics in the UK/EU/EEA, and the ICO has directly clarified (per guidance obtained in April 2025) that Advanced Consent Mode's "cookieless pings" do not themselves provide a lawful basis for tracking non-consenting users — meaning Kept needs a proper Consent Management Platform blocking GA4 and ad tags until consent is affirmatively given, with a "Reject All" option as prominent as "Accept All"[^43][^44]. Separately, the UK's Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, in force from 5 February 2026, creates a narrow analytics-cookie exemption from consent requirements, but only if the cookie is used solely for the site's own analytics, does not follow users across other sites, is not shared with third parties, and visitors can still opt out — GA4 does not qualify for this exemption by default and requires disabling Google Signals and all cross-service data sharing to potentially qualify[^45]. Given this complexity and Kept's small scale, the pragmatic approach is a compliant CMP with Consent Mode v2 properly wired to GA4 and Google Ads, rather than attempting to rely on the new analytics exemption.

A sensible UTM scheme for a £1,000/month operation should use consistent, lowercase parameters: `utm_source` (google, bing, meta), `utm_medium` (cpc, organic, referral), `utm_campaign` (segment-city, e.g. probate-manchester, chainbreak-leeds), and `utm_content` for ad variant testing — kept simple enough that a two-person founder team can maintain it without a dedicated analytics resource. For incrementality at this budget, formal geo-holdout or lift testing is unlikely to be statistically practical given low weekly lead volumes (single digits per week per Section 7's model); the more realistic approach for a small advertiser is disciplined channel-level cost-per-lead tracking combined with a simple new-lead intake question ("how did you hear about us") to sense-check platform-reported attribution against self-reported source, cross-referenced against call-tracking numbers assigned per channel[^46].

## 9. Negative Keywords and Brand Safety

Given the overlap between "buy" and "sell" terminology across property and unrelated markets, a solid negative-keyword base for this category should exclude: rental terms ("rent," "to let," "letting agent"), employment terms ("jobs," "careers," "hiring," since "we buy any house" phrasing can trigger unrelated "buy" searches), vehicle-related terms ("we buy any car," "car buyer," given the well-known unrelated brand using similar phrasing), US-specific real-estate terms and currency references ("realtor," "MLS," "dollars"), and generic informational/DIY intent terms ("how to sell my house myself," "estate agent fees calculator") that indicate research rather than transaction-ready intent. On brand safety, Kept's ads should avoid appearing in placements adjacent to breaking news coverage of repossession court cases, bereavement/death-related news content, or divorce-related celebrity news, since juxtaposition against real distress content in a news feed risks appearing exploitative — this is a reasonable extension of the brand's own stated "calm certainty, never urgency" voice rather than a documented regulatory requirement, and can be operationally managed via standard Display/News placement exclusion lists and topic exclusions available in Google Ads' content settings.
